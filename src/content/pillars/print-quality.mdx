Most FDM print quality problems trace back to one of four things: temperature, flow, motion, or the interface between the part and the bed. Once you can place a defect into one of those buckets, fixing it stops being guesswork.

This page is the map. It groups the common defects by what's actually going wrong, links to a full walkthrough for each one, and gives you a quick way to narrow down where to start looking on your own printer.

## Start with what you can see

Before changing any settings, look closely at the failed print (or the one in progress) and match it to one of these categories:

- **The surface looks rough or has fine wispy strands between parts.** That's almost always [stringing](/print-quality/stringing/), caused by filament oozing during travel moves.
- **Corners lift off the bed, or the part warps into a curved shape.** That's [warping](/print-quality/warping/), driven by uneven cooling and shrinkage.
- **You can clearly see and feel ridges running around the whole model, even where you didn't expect them.** That's [visible layer lines](/print-quality/layer-lines/), a mix of mechanical and cooling causes.
- **The first layer won't stick, peels up at the edges, or the whole print detaches partway through.** Start with [first-layer adhesion](/print-quality/first-layer-adhesion/).
- **The part is missing material in some spots, or has blobs and zits in others.** That's an extrusion balance issue; see [under-extrusion vs. over-extrusion](/print-quality/under-extrusion-vs-over-extrusion/).
- **The base of the print bulges outward wider than the rest of the model.** That's [elephant's foot](/print-quality/elephants-foot/), caused by the still-soft first layers squishing under the weight of the part above them.
- **Extrusion stopped entirely, or filament grinds without coming out.** That's most likely a [nozzle clog](/print-quality/nozzle-clogs/), not a settings problem.

<Callout kind="tip" label="Change one variable at a time">
It's tempting to adjust temperature, retraction, and cooling all in the same test print. When two changes fix the problem, you won't know which one actually mattered — and you'll carry an unnecessary setting into every future print. Isolate variables, even when it's slower.
</Callout>

## The four root causes

**Temperature.** Too hot and the filament oozes, sags, and strings. Too cold and it doesn't bond between layers or extrude consistently. Every polymer has a usable range, and it shifts a few degrees between filament brands even within the same material (see the [PLA vs. PETG vs. ABS](/materials/pla-vs-petg-vs-abs/) comparison for typical ranges).

**Flow.** This is the relationship between how much filament your firmware _thinks_ it's pushing and how much is actually coming out of the nozzle. A miscalibrated E-steps value or an incorrect flow percentage throws off wall thickness, layer adhesion, and dimensional accuracy at the same time, even when everything else is dialed in. [Calibrating flow rate](/calibration/flow-rate/) is one of the highest-leverage things you can do on a new printer.

**Motion.** Retraction, travel speed, acceleration, and jerk/junction settings all affect how cleanly the nozzle moves between features without dragging plastic with it. A retraction setting that works for one filament can under- or over-perform on another with different viscosity.

**Bed interface.** Everything from bed leveling to surface material to the first layer height affects whether a part actually stays where you put it. This is also the category most likely to produce failures that look like something else — a print that "warps" at one corner might really be a bed that's not level there.

## A troubleshooting order that saves time

When a print is coming out wrong and you're not sure why, checking things in this order avoids fixing symptoms instead of causes:

1. **Confirm the bed is actually level and the first layer squish is correct.** A large share of "random" print failures are bed issues in disguise.
2. **Check flow calibration**, especially if the printer is new, the nozzle was recently changed, or you switched filament brands.
3. **Verify nozzle temperature against the filament you're using**, not just the printer's default profile. Manufacturer-recommended ranges vary and your particular spool may run better a few degrees off the middle of that range.
4. **Look at retraction and travel behavior** if the surface finish issue is stringing- or blob-specific rather than structural.
5. **Only then tune cooling and speed** for cosmetic surface finish, once the structural fundamentals are solid.

Doing them out of order usually means re-doing an earlier step later, because a bed or flow problem can masquerade as a cooling or retraction problem.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">
Copying someone else's slicer profile wholesale. Retraction distance, flow percentage, and even effective nozzle temperature are specific to your combination of printer, extruder, nozzle, and filament spool. A profile is a reasonable starting point, not a finished answer.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake">
Chasing a cosmetic issue with structural settings. Fuzzy skin, ironing, and cooling fan tuning make a print look better without addressing why the underlying geometry is inconsistent. Fix flow and temperature first.
</Callout>

## Problems that aren't a "settings" issue at all

Most of the defects above are fixed with a slicer setting or a calibration pass. Two common ones aren't:

**Nozzle clogs** stop or restrict extrusion regardless of what the slicer is told to do — no flow or temperature adjustment fixes plastic that physically can't get through the nozzle. See [nozzle clogs](/print-quality/nozzle-clogs/) for the cold-pull procedure that actually clears one.

**Elephant's foot** (the base of a print flaring outward) looks like an extrusion or adhesion problem but is really about how much weight is pressing down on still-soft first layers, and how long they stay soft under a hot bed. See [elephant's foot](/print-quality/elephants-foot/) for the fix, which is closer to bed-leveling and first-layer-height territory than a flow adjustment.

Work through the guides below in whichever order matches what you're actually seeing, or start from [calibration](/calibration/) if you're setting up a printer for the first time and haven't had a failure yet.
