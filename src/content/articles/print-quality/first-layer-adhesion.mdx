A print that won't stick, peels up at the edges, or detaches part-way through almost always comes down to one of three things: the nozzle-to-bed distance, the bed surface and temperature, or the material's inherent adhesion behavior. Work through them in this order.

## Step 1: Check nozzle-to-bed distance

This is the single most common cause of adhesion problems, and it's worth ruling out first even if you leveled the bed recently.

- **Too far away**: the first layer looks stringy, round, and doesn't flatten into the bed — you can see individual extruded lines with gaps between them.
- **Too close**: the nozzle drags, the filament can't come out fast enough, and you'll often hear a clicking or grinding sound as the extruder skips.
- **Correct**: the first layer looks slightly flattened and matte, with lines touching their neighbors, but not squished into a solid sheet.

See the full [bed leveling guide](/calibration/bed-leveling/) for how to set this properly across the whole bed, not just the center.

## Step 2: Match bed temperature to the material

Every material has a bed temperature range that helps the first layers stay soft and bond to the surface long enough to build a mechanical grip:

- PLA: bed surface generally works well in the 50–60°C range.
- PETG: usually 70–80°C.
- ABS/ASA: usually 90–110°C, which is also why these materials benefit most from an enclosure — a cooler surrounding air temperature pulls heat out of the bed and the part faster than the bed heater can replace it.

If adhesion is inconsistent between prints on the same settings, confirm the bed is actually reaching its set temperature before the print starts, especially on larger beds that take longer to heat evenly.

## Step 3: Match the surface (and any adhesive) to the material

Most bed surfaces are designed around specific materials:

- Smooth or textured PEI sheets work well for PLA and PETG without additional adhesive in most cases.
- Glass beds often need a thin layer of glue stick or a dedicated adhesion spray, particularly for ABS and PETG.
- PETG has a tendency to bond too well to bare PEI in some cases, which can pull small chunks off the sheet's surface on removal — a light release agent (glue stick works) is a normal precaution for PETG on PEI, not a sign something is wrong.

## Step 4: Add mechanical help for large or corner-heavy parts

If leveling, temperature, and surface are all correct and adhesion is still marginal — usually for large flat parts or shapes with sharp corners — a brim adds surface area exactly where lifting starts. See [warping](/print-quality/warping/) for why corners lift in the first place; a brim is treating the same root cause from the adhesion side rather than the thermal side.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Adding more adhesive or a brim before checking bed leveling and temperature. Adhesives and brims compensate for a marginal bond — they don't fix a nozzle gap that's genuinely too large, and stacking fixes makes it harder to tell what actually solved the problem.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="tip" label="Clean the surface first">
Oils from handling (even just touching the bed with bare hands) can reduce adhesion noticeably on PEI and glass surfaces. Wiping the bed with isopropyl alcohol before a print that needs strong adhesion is a fast, free troubleshooting step that's easy to skip.
</Callout>

## Brim, raft, or skirt: they solve different problems

These three are often lumped together as "adhesion helpers," but they're not interchangeable:

- **Skirt** doesn't touch the part at all — it's a printed outline a short distance away, used to prime the nozzle and give you a visual check that filament is extruding evenly before the actual print starts. It does nothing for adhesion.
- **Brim** is a flat, single-layer border connected directly to the part's base, adding surface area exactly where corners tend to lift. It's the right choice for a part that's otherwise fine but has marginal adhesion at the edges, and it peels off cleanly after printing.
- **Raft** is a full sacrificial base layer printed underneath the entire part, with the model printing on top of it rather than directly on the bed. It's the most aggressive option — useful for parts with very little first-layer contact area (thin legs, small footprints) or a bed surface that's known to be uneven — but it costs more material and time, and leaves a rougher bottom surface once removed.

Reach for a brim first for corner-lifting on an otherwise-adhering print; reach for a raft only when the part's actual contact area with the bed is the problem, not just the edges.

## Bed surfaces compared

| Surface | Works well for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Smooth PEI | PLA, PETG | Good default; PETG may bond too strongly without a release agent |
| Textured PEI | PLA, PETG, ABS | Slightly rougher bottom finish; more forgiving on adhesion |
| Glass | PLA, PETG (with adhesive) | Usually needs glue stick or spray for anything but PLA |
| Spring steel + PEI coating | All common materials | Flexes to pop parts off; the coating itself matters more than the steel |

The surface matters less than matching temperature and leveling to whatever surface you actually have — a good surface can't compensate for an incorrect nozzle-to-bed gap.
