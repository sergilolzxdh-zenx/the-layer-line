Under-extrusion and over-extrusion are opposite problems that sometimes get confused because both can produce a rough, inconsistent-looking print. The distinction matters because the fixes are opposite too: treating one as the other makes it worse.

## Quick diagnostic: the single-wall test

Print a simple single-walled cylinder or box with no top or bottom layers. This isolates flow from every other variable: no infill pattern, no top-layer bridging, just one line of extrusion stacked repeatedly.

- Walls that are **thinner than the nozzle diameter**, with visible gaps or a "see-through" quality when held up to light, point to under-extrusion.
- Walls that are **noticeably thicker than the nozzle diameter** or have excess material squeezing out at the corners point to over-extrusion.

Measure the actual wall thickness with calipers and compare it to your nozzle diameter. For a 0.4 mm nozzle printing a single-wall test, you're looking for wall thickness close to 0.4–0.45 mm, not exactly one to the thousandth, but close enough that a large deviation is informative.

## Why both can look "rough"

Extrusion volume that's off in either direction disrupts how cleanly each new line bonds to its neighbor. Under-extrusion leaves gaps that show up as visible texture and weak points; over-extrusion pushes excess material sideways with nowhere to go, which also shows up as texture, just in the form of bumps and blobs instead of gaps. From a few feet away, both can read as "the surface looks bad." That's exactly why the single-wall test matters more than eyeballing the finished part.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Adjusting nozzle temperature to fix what's actually a flow rate problem. Temperature affects how easily plastic flows once it's moving, but the volume of plastic moved is set by flow rate (and, upstream of that, E-steps) — not temperature. If walls are the wrong thickness on a well-tuned printer, start with flow, not heat.
</Callout>
