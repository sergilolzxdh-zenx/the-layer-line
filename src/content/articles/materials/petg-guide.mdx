Most PETG frustration comes from using a PLA-tuned printer profile and being surprised when the results are worse, not better, than PLA. PETG isn't harder to print than PLA — it just needs different answers to the same settings.

## Starting settings

- **Nozzle temperature**: 230–250°C, brand-dependent. Start in the middle of your specific spool's recommended range and adjust based on stringing and layer adhesion, not the other way around.
- **Bed temperature**: 70–80°C. A PEI or similar textured surface generally works well.
- **Cooling fan**: lower than PLA, especially on the first several layers. PETG benefits from staying slightly warmer for longer to bond layers well — full PLA-level cooling from layer one is one of the more common causes of both weak layer adhesion and paradoxically worse-looking stringing.
- **Retraction**: PETG tends to need more careful tuning than PLA, not necessarily more retraction distance. See [retraction tuning](/calibration/retraction-tuning/) to find your actual threshold rather than assuming a higher number automatically helps.

## The two problems people associate with PETG

**Stringing.** PETG's molten behavior holds thin strings together more easily than PLA's does at a comparable position in its temperature range. This is addressed through the same two levers as any stringing problem — temperature and retraction — see the full [stringing guide](/print-quality/stringing/). PETG usually ends up printing well toward the lower end of its temperature range specifically because of this tendency.

**Bed adhesion that's too strong.** On bare PEI surfaces in particular, PETG can bond so well that removing the part risks damaging the sheet's coating. A thin layer of glue stick or a dedicated release agent isn't a workaround for bad adhesion — for PETG on PEI, it's closer to standard practice.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">
Copying a PLA cooling profile directly. High fan speed from the first layer is a common PLA default that actively works against PETG's layer adhesion and can make stringing look worse rather than better.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake">
Assuming rough surface finish means the temperature is wrong. PETG absorbs moisture from the air more readily than PLA, and damp filament produces a rough, sometimes bubbly surface finish that looks like a temperature problem but is actually a storage problem. If PETG that printed cleanly before starts looking rough, drying the filament is worth trying before adjusting temperature.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="tip" label="Dry filament before a long or important print">
A few hours in a filament dryer or a low-temperature oven (following the specific manufacturer's guidance for safe temperatures) before a print that needs to come out right the first time is cheap insurance against moisture-related surface defects.
</Callout>

## Drying PETG properly

PETG is more hygroscopic than PLA, meaning it pulls moisture from the air faster and more persistently, so "leave it out overnight" is rarely enough for filament that's been open for weeks. A practical baseline is 4–6 hours at 60–65°C in a dedicated filament dryer, or a household oven set as low as it goes and monitored closely if a dryer isn't available — check the specific manufacturer's guidance first, since ovens can overshoot low target temperatures and some spool materials deform below PETG's own softening point. Signs the filament was actually wet before drying: audible popping or crackling at the nozzle, a rough or slightly frosted surface texture, and inconsistent stringing that doesn't track with any retraction change.

## Annealing for extra heat resistance

PETG's out-of-the-box heat resistance (softening around 70–80°C) can be improved somewhat by annealing: baking a finished, cooled part at a controlled temperature below its glass transition point for a period of time, which allows internal stresses to relax and crystallinity to increase slightly. This is a genuine trade-off, not a free upgrade — annealing can cause a finished part to shrink or warp slightly as internal stress releases, so it's worth testing on a non-critical print first, and it matters much less for parts that never encounter elevated temperatures anyway.

<Callout kind="tip" label="An enclosure isn't required, but changes cooling behavior">
PETG doesn't need an enclosure to print successfully, but if you already have one (for ABS/ASA prints), be aware that trapped ambient heat can make PETG's already-lower cooling needs even lower — a print that looks slightly under-cooled with normal fan settings outside an enclosure may need more, not less, cooling inside one.
</Callout>
